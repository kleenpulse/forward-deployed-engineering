Forward Deployed Engineering · Lesson 9 · Stage 9 · about 60 minutes

Telemetry, ROI and customer health

The pilot has run for four weeks, and the renewal meeting is on Monday. Dana wants four answers: what it costs, whether customers get replies faster, whether the team really uses it, and whether it is working right now. All four are in the agent's telemetry and the helpdesk. So are four easy ways to get each one wrong.

Words with a dotted underline have a plain-English definition. Hover, tap or tab to them. All of them are in the jargon decoder.

Your win today

Four SQL queries over a month of OpenTelemetry spans and helpdesk tickets that give Dana honest numbers: cost per team (each token counted once, priced per model, cache included, without your own eval traffic), the agreed success metric against its baseline, adoption by week, and an alert that catches a failure that raised no errors, without paging anyone on a quiet Sunday. Then you write the one-page note for the renewal meeting.

1 · Four questions before the renewal

OpenAI's FDE job ad says your success is measured "through production adoption, measurable workflow impact, and eval-driven feedback".OpenAI, Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) job ad, "About the role", as of November 2026. Lesson 8 was the eval part. This lesson is the other two: does Acme use it, and does it change their work?

Enterprise software is sold for a period and then renewed, or not. The people who look after accounts sum up the risk in a customer health score. Gainsight, which sells software for this, defines it as "a predictive metric that helps Customer Success teams evaluate the likelihood of renewal, growth, or churn", built from inputs "like product usage, support history, NPS, and engagement".Gainsight, "Customer health scores". A vendor's own definition, so read it as one view, not a standard. As the FDE, you own the usage part, and you are often the first person to see it go wrong.

Dana's four questions, and the trap in each:

QuestionThe tempting answerThe honest one
What does it cost, per team?Add up every token on every spanTokens from model-call spans only, priced per model, cache included, live traffic only

2 · Traces and spans

To answer any of this you need a record of every run. OpenTelemetry is the open standard for that. A trace is "The path of a request through your application", and "A span represents a unit of work or operation. Spans are the building blocks of Traces." Spans nest: "child spans represent sub-operations."OpenTelemetry, "Traces" and its section "Spans".

For AI systems, OpenTelemetry's GenAI semantic conventions give the spans and attributes standard names. A model call is named "{gen_ai.operation.name} {gen_ai.request.model}", a tool call "execute_tool {gen_ai.tool.name}", and an agent run "invoke_agent {gen_ai.agent.name}".OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions, release v0.1.0: inference, tool and agent spans. The app.* span attributes are Acme's own, added by hand on the root span. The conventions encourage that: "Application developers are encouraged to follow this semantic convention for tools invoked by their own code and to manually instrument any tool calls that automatic instrumentations do not cover." Decide on day one which questions you'll need to answer (which team, which ticket, live or test) and put them on the root span. You can't add them to last month's traces.

Three warnings before you build on these names:

  • They are still changing. Every page is marked "Status: Development". In 2026 the GenAI conventions moved to their own repository, and its first release renamed metrics, for example replacing the gen_ai.client.token.usage histogram. Write down the version you instrument against.semantic-conventions-genai CHANGELOG, v0.1.0, "Breaking changes". The old pages on opentelemetry.io now say "This page has moved and is no longer maintained in this repository."
  • Prompts and replies are personal data. "Model instructions, user messages, and model outputs are considered sensitive", and instrumentations "SHOULD NOT capture them by default, but SHOULD provide an option for users to opt in."GenAI spans, "Capturing instructions, inputs and outputs". The attribute gen_ai.input.messages "is likely to contain sensitive information including user/PII data." Turn content capture on only where the contract allows it. Counts, ids and statuses answer most questions without it.
  • Tokens are recorded twice. More on that next.

3 · What it costs, per team

Providers bill by tokens and quote prices per million tokens (MTok).Anthropic, "Pricing": model prices are listed per "MTok", and "All prices are in USD". Checked November 2026; prices change, so read them from the page, not from a lesson. So cost is arithmetic on token counts. Three details make it come out wrong:

The same tokens sit on more than one span. Look at the root span above: the agent framework copied the run's total onto it. The conventions warn about exactly this: "Don't sum gen_ai.usage.* over all spans without filtering by operation name. The same tokens can be reported on multiple types of spans: agent and inference spans, or inference spans from gateways and different layers of the inference infrastructure."GenAI token metrics, "How to aggregate token usage". It also suggests the token metrics over spans where you can, because "They aren't affected by trace sampling." Count tokens from the chat spans only.

Cached tokens cost less. With prompt caching, the provider bills the part of the prompt it has seen recently, such as the long system prompt, at a fraction of the input price. Anthropic lists "Cache read (hit) | 0.1x base input price on most models", and adds "What a cache hit costs depends on the model", so keep the multiplier per model.Anthropic, "Pricing", "Prompt caching". Writing to the cache costs more than a normal input token: "5-minute cache write | 1.25x base input price". The exercise has cache reads only. Then check what your count includes. The conventions say gen_ai.usage.input_tokens "SHOULD include cached tokens". Anthropic's API counts the other way, so the conventions' mapping for it reads: "Anthropic input_tokens excludes cached tokens. Compute: gen_ai.usage.input_tokens = input_tokens + cache_read_input_tokens + cache_write_input_tokens".GenAI conventions, Anthropic page. Langfuse, an LLM observability tool, has the same warning: "Inclusive counts must be converted into exclusive buckets before they are stored" (Langfuse, "Model Usage & Cost Tracking"). Get this wrong and you bill every cached token twice.

Your own traffic isn't the customer's usage. Your Lesson 8 eval ran against production twice: 96 runs. Tag them (app.source=eval) and report them on their own line. Otherwise they make the support team look busier than it is.

At Acme the honest total is USD 6.66 for 1,050 live runs in four weeks. The naive query says USD 35,248.28. Either number goes into a slide. Only one survives the customer's finance team checking the invoice.

4 · Did it save time? Measure what was agreed

In Lesson 2 Dana and Luis agreed one success metric: the median time to the first response for order-status emails, from the helpdesk, with a baseline of 22 hours. Report that, measured the same way. Not the average, which a few week-long waits pull up. Not all emails, because the agent only drafts order-status replies.

One trap hides in "measured the same way". Some emails have no response yet. Leave them out and the median improves, because you dropped the slowest cases. They have waited at least until the moment you run the report, so count that wait. At Acme, 27 of 830 emails are still open. Dropping them moves the median from 2.62 to 2.51 hours: small this month, but in a bad week it is the whole story.

Why not just ask people? Because people are bad at judging their own speed. In METR's 2025 study, experienced developers using AI tools "take 19% longer to complete issues". Yet "developers expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even after experiencing the slowdown, they still believed AI had sped them up by 20%."METR, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity". METR now marks the speed results as "out of date", and its 2026 follow-up repeats that self-reported speedups "can be quite unreliable". The point here is the gap between felt and measured, not how fast AI makes anyone today. Measured results can be large and real. A study of 5,179 customer support agents found an AI assistant raised "issues resolved per hour, by 14% on average, including a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers".Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" (NBER working paper 31161; published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025), abstract. That number came from the helpdesk data, not from a survey. Do the same: take the number from the system of record, and quote what people say as colour, not as proof.

Then turn time into money only with numbers the customer gives you. If Acme says an hour of a support agent costs EUR 40, you can multiply. If you make up the hourly cost, the ROI slide falls apart at the first question.

5 · Adoption, not activity

"The agent answered 90% of emails" describes the agent. It says nothing about whether anyone used the answers. At Acme the agent writes a draft, and a person sends it or writes their own. The adoption number is the share of order-status emails sent with the agent's draft. In week one that was 56%, not 90%.

Run counts, token totals and "emails touched" are vanity metrics: they rise with use, but also with retries, tests and errors. Gainsight's own table says usage inputs like "Feature adoption, login frequency" are there because they show "value realization". Count the step where a person chose to use the output.

Now look at Acme's weeks: 56%, 58%, 69%, then 49%. Week 4 fell by 20 points. Nobody complained, and the error rate was zero. Something broke, and the next section is how you'd have known on the first day.

6 · Alert on what customers feel

Google's SRE book names the four golden signals: "latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. If you can only measure four metrics of your user-facing system, focus on these four." Its definition of errors includes requests that fail "implicitly (for example, an HTTP 200 success response, but coupled with the wrong content)".Google, Site Reliability Engineering, chapter 6, "Monitoring Distributed Systems", "The Four Golden Signals". That is a silent failure, and agents produce them all the time.

Here is Acme's. On 17 November the order system's new release started sending a status code nobody had mapped. The MCP server passed it on as unknown, every call succeeded, and the agent did the safe thing: it handed the email to a person. Nothing raised an error. The share of runs handed off jumped from about 4–8% a day to 39–53%, and stayed there for four days until Dana's numbers showed the drop in adoption.

The SRE book separates "what's broken" (the symptom) from "why" (the cause), and advises: "it's better to spend much more effort on catching symptoms than causes".Google SRE book, chapter 6, "Symptoms Versus Causes" and "Tying These Principles Together". Acme's symptom is "emails the agent couldn't answer". An alert on it catches both of the month's incidents: the planned outage on 3 November, which threw errors, and the silent one, which didn't. An alert on error spans catches only the first.

An alert on errors also fires when nothing is wrong. On two quiet Sundays, one tool call failed and the retry worked. No customer noticed, but with four runs that day the error share was over 5%, so someone would have been paged. The book: "Every page should be actionable", and "If a page merely merits a robotic response, it shouldn't be a page." So the alert needs enough traffic to mean something. Today's rule: more than 20% of live runs handed off, on a day with at least 20 runs.

The grown-up version: SLOs. An SLI is "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service that is provided", usually "the number of good events divided by the total number of events". An SLO is "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI", and "the error budget is 100% minus the SLO".Google SRE book, "Service Level Objectives"; SRE workbook, "Implementing SLOs" and "Alerting on SLOs". For Acme: SLI = share of live runs the agent answered; SLO = 90% per week; error budget = 10%. Instead of a fixed daily threshold, you alert on the burn rate, "how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget". On 17 November Acme was handing off about half of all runs, five times the 10% budget, so the week's budget was gone in about a day and a half. Start with the simple daily rule; move to SLOs when the customer wants a number in the contract.

Fix the four functions in start.ts. The TODO list in the file follows the checks. Give yourself 30 minutes before you open the solution.

  1. Take tokens from chat spans only.
  2. Price each chat span with its own model's prices, per million tokens, with cached input at the cache rate. Pass PRICES in as a query parameter.
  3. Join each chat span to its run's root span to get the team. Live runs only.
  4. The median for order-status emails, counting unanswered ones until REPORT_TIME.
  5. Adoption per week: the share of order-status tickets with draft = 'used'.
  6. Alert per day when more than 20% of live runs were handed off…
  7. …and only on days with at least 20 live runs.

10 · Write the renewal note

Dana takes one page into Monday's meeting, and will forward it to the CFO. Write it from your numbers. It saves as you type.

The fifth check is the one people skip. It feels safer to leave the incident out of a renewal note. Don't. Dana will hear about it anyway, and an account that hears it from you, with the fix already in place, trusts you more than one that hears it from the support team.
