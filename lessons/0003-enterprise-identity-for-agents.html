Forward Deployed Engineering · Lesson 3 · Stage 1 · about 60 minutes

Enterprise identity for agents

In your own apps you own the login. At an enterprise customer, their IT team owns it. Learn how their staff sign in, how accounts appear and disappear, and how to make sure an agent can never see more than the person it works for.

Words with a dotted underline have a plain-English definition. Hover, tap or tab to them. All of them are in the jargon decoder.

Your win today

By the end you can explain to a customer's IT team, in their own words, how their staff will sign in, how accounts are created and removed, and exactly what the agent can see. You will also have written a Postgres row-level security policy that stops an agent's query from reading another department's or another customer's rows, checked by seven automatic tests.

1 · Your login versus their login

You already know how login works in an app you build. You keep a users table, hash passwords, set a session cookie, rotate refresh tokens, and a route guard checks the session before a page loads. You own every part of it.

At an enterprise customer, almost none of that is yours. The customer already has an identity provider, such as Okta, Microsoft Entra ID or Google Workspace, that holds every employee's account. Their IT team will not create a second set of passwords for your app. They expect your app, and your agent, to plug into what they already run.

Two words will keep you straight. Authentication answers "who are you?". Authorization answers "what may you do?". Your app's login form did both at once. In an enterprise they come apart, and each has its own standard. IT will ask you five questions, and each answer has a name:

IT asksYour answerCalled
How will our staff sign in?Through your identity provider. No new passwords.SSO (via SAML or OIDC)
How are accounts created and removed?Your identity provider pushes every change to us.SCIM
Who can do what?A few roles, mapped from your groups.RBAC
Where is that enforced?In the database, on every query.Row-level security
What can the agent do, and as whom?Only what the signed-in person can, with a narrow token for each tool.Delegated, scoped tokens

The rest of the lesson takes these one at a time, then you build the fourth one for real.

2 · SSO: SAML or OIDC, and what IT will ask you for

With SSO, your app stops checking passwords. It sends the person to the customer's identity provider, the provider signs them in, and it sends your app a signed statement saying who they are. In SAML's terms, the identity provider is "the entity providing the identities, including the ability to authenticate a user", and your app is the service provider, "the entity providing the service, typically in the form of an app".Both definitions from Okta Developer, "Understand SAML".

There are two standards for that signed statement, and enterprise IT teams support one or both:

  • SAML is the older one. The statement is an XML document called a SAML assertion. The browser posts it to an address on your app.
  • OIDC "is a simple identity layer on top of the OAuth 2.0 protocol".OpenID Connect Core 1.0, Abstract. Section 2 defines the ID token as "a security token that contains Claims about the Authentication of an End-User", "represented as a JSON Web Token (JWT)". The statement is an ID token, which is a JWT. It is the OAuth redirect flow you may already know, plus that token.

You don't need to implement either protocol by hand: a library does that. What you need is to know which values to swap with IT, because that is the whole setup meeting. Here is what Microsoft Entra ID asks for. Other providers use similar names.

You give ITIT gives you

Sign on URL.Login URL, their Microsoft Entra Identifier, and a signing certificate so you can check the assertion really came from them.Field names from Microsoft Learn, "Enable SAML single sign-on for an enterprise application".

Its sample discovery document lists client_secret_post and private_key_jwt as the ways a server can prove who it is.

Support whichever one the customer's IT team runs. If you get to choose, OIDC is closer to what you know: JSON, JWTs and OAuth redirects.

When an ID token arrives, a library validates it, but you should know what it checks. The token claims that matter most are iss (who issued it), sub (who it is about), aud (who it is for) and exp (when it expires). The issuer from the discovery document "MUST exactly match the value of the iss (issuer) Claim", and the token must be rejected if its audience does not include your client ID.OpenID Connect Core 1.0, section 3.1.3.7, "ID Token Validation". Then key the person in your database by issuer plus sub, not by email. The spec defines sub as "a locally unique and never reassigned identifier within the Issuer for the End-User". An email address can change or be reused for a new hire.

3 · SCIM: the day someone leaves

SSO only acts at sign-in. Say Lee leaves on Friday and IT disables Lee in the identity provider. Lee can't start a new sign-in. But the session cookie and refresh token your app already gave Lee are yours, not the identity provider's, so they keep working. An agent job running on Lee's behalf keeps running too. Even Microsoft's own sign-out ends the session in one app while the user "may still be signed in to other applications".Microsoft Learn, OIDC on the Microsoft identity platform, "Single sign-out". That's why it offers a front-channel logout address that each app must handle itself. Closing that gap is what SCIM is for.

SCIM is "an HTTP-based protocol that makes managing identities in multi-domain scenarios easier".RFC 7644, SCIM Protocol, Abstract. The user and group schemas, including the active attribute, are in RFC 7643, section 4.1.1: "A Boolean value indicating the user's administrative status." In practice your app exposes a small REST API, /Users and /Groups, and the customer's identity provider calls it. You don't call them; they call you.

  • Provisioning. A new hire is assigned to your app, and the identity provider creates their user in your app before they ever sign in.
  • Updates. Someone moves from Support to Finance, and your copy of their department changes.
  • Deprovisioning. Microsoft Entra ID usually disables before it deletes: "For SCIM applications, a disable is a request to set the active property to false". A user deleted in Entra is permanently deleted 30 days later, and only then does Entra send a DELETE request to your app.Microsoft Learn, "How Application Provisioning works in Microsoft Entra ID", section "Deprovisioning". It also recommends: "always support both soft-deletes and hard-deletes." After a DELETE, the SCIM spec says you answer 204 (No Content) and from then on "MUST return a 404 (Not Found) error code for all operations associated with the previously deleted resource".RFC 7644, section 3.6, "Deleting Resources".

So when your SCIM endpoint receives active: false, a database flag isn't enough. End their sessions, revoke their refresh tokens, stop every agent job running for them, and make sure the database stops returning their rows. The exercise checks that last part: Lee is in the data with active = false.

4 · RBAC: roles, not people

RBAC is the plain idea that "each user is assigned one or more roles, and each role is assigned one or more privileges".NIST, Role Based Access Control project. The NIST model became an American National Standard in 2004. Nobody gets permissions one by one. In an enterprise, the roles usually come from the customer's groups. IT assigns a group to your app in the identity provider, and Entra provisions or deprovisions people "based on whether they're members of a group that's assigned to the application".Same Entra provisioning page, "Assignment-based scoping". Your job is a short mapping: group Finance-Staff becomes role member in the Finance department, and group IT-Admins becomes admin. Keep it to a few roles that IT can understand without you.

One word, two meanings. In this lesson "role" means two different things. An RBAC role (member, admin) is a business idea, stored as data in your users table. A database role is the identity a connection uses inside Postgres. The agent uses one database role, agent, for everyone. The human's RBAC role is something the database looks up.

5 · Enforce it in the database: row-level security

An agent composes queries for you, through tools you write. If the only thing that keeps Finance's agent out of HR's files is a WHERE clause in each tool, or a line in the prompt, one forgotten clause leaks everything. Row-level security moves that filter into Postgres itself, so it applies to every query on the table, whoever wrote it.

Three facts from the Postgres docs do most of the work:

  • Default deny. Once RLS is on for a table, every row read or changed "must be allowed by a row security policy". "If no policy exists for the table, a default-deny policy is used, meaning that no rows are visible or can be modified."
  • Some roles skip it. "Superusers and roles with the BYPASSRLS attribute always bypass the row security system when accessing a table. Table owners normally bypass row security as well", unless you add FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY. So the agent must connect as its own plain role, never as the owner.
  • Policies add up. When several policies apply, permissive ones (the default) combine with OR and restrictive ones with AND.All three quotes from PostgreSQL docs, "Row Security Policies". A policy's USING expression filters the rows a command can see. WITH CHECK limits the rows it can write.

The policy must know who the agent is working for. The common pattern: before each query, your server writes the signed-in person's id into a setting, and the policy reads it back.

  • set_config('app.user_id', id, true) sets it. With true, "the new value will only apply during the current transaction".
  • current_setting('app.user_id', true) reads it. The true means "return NULL instead of an error if it was never set".PostgreSQL docs, "System Administration Functions", Configuration Settings Functions.

Keep the setting transaction-local. Servers reuse database connections from a connection pool, so a setting that outlives its transaction could carry Ana's id into Ben's next request. And the id must come from the token your server verified, never from anything the model wrote.

6 · The agent twist: act as the human, not as a god

The quick way to wire up an agent is one service account that can read everything, plus a prompt that says what not to show. That agent can reach the sum of everyone's data, and one clever question or one buggy tool is enough to leak it. (In Lesson 2, Northfield's pilot read order data through a service account. That works while every user may see every row it reads. Once different people may see different rows, the agent needs to know who it is working for.) The safer rule: the agent acts with the signed-in person's permissions, and each tool gets a token that can do only that tool's job.

MCP, the standard way to connect agents to tools, writes this down in its authorization spec. The current version is dated 2026-07-28:MCP specification 2026-07-28, "Authorization". It builds on the OAuth 2.1 draft plus several RFCs, including RFC 8707 (resource indicators) and RFC 9728 (protected resource metadata).

  • Authorization is optional, and depends on the transport. Servers reached over HTTP "SHOULD conform to this specification". Local servers over STDIO "SHOULD NOT follow this specification, and instead retrieve credentials from the environment". In practice, a local tool with an API key in an environment variable acts as whoever owns that key, which is a service account. Ask whose key it is.
  • The client acts for a person. The MCP server is an OAuth 2.1 resource server, and the MCP client makes requests "on behalf of a resource owner": the human.
  • Each token belongs to one server. The client must name the server it wants a token for (the resource parameter). The server "MUST validate that access tokens were issued specifically for them as the intended audience" and "MUST NOT accept or transit any other tokens".
  • No passthrough. When your MCP server calls the customer's ticketing system, it needs a separate token for that system. Forwarding the user's token is "token passthrough", which the spec "explicitly" forbids. It breaks audit trails and lets a stolen token reach every service that would accept it.MCP, "Security Best Practices", sections "Token Passthrough" ("MCP servers MUST NOT accept any tokens that were not explicitly issued for the MCP server") and "Scope Minimization".
  • Narrow OAuth scopes, then step up. Clients "SHOULD follow the principle of least privilege by requesting only the scopes necessary". When a token lacks a scope, the server answers 403 with error="insufficient_scope" and the scope it needs, and the client asks the person for more. The best-practices guide warns against broad scopes like files:*, db:* and admin:*, because a stolen broad token reaches everything they cover.

How does a server get that separate, narrower token and still say who it's for? One standard answer is OAuth token exchange. It trades a subject_token ("the party on behalf of whom the request is being made") and optionally an actor_token ("the acting party") for a new token. A field in the new token, act, records that "delegation has occurred", so logs show both the human and the agent.RFC 8693, OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, sections 1.1 and 2.1. It separates delegation (the agent keeps its own identity) from impersonation (the agent becomes the user). Whether the customer's identity provider supports it is a question for IT.

Put together, one request looks like this:

  1. Ana signs in through SSO. Your server validates her ID token and knows she is u-ana.
  2. Ana asks the agent a question. The agent calls the documents tool with an access token whose audience is that tool's server and whose scope is read-only.
  3. The tool's server validates the token, opens a transaction, runs set_config('app.user_id', 'u-ana', true), switches to the agent database role and runs the query.
  4. Postgres applies the RLS policy and returns only rows Ana may see. SCIM keeps her department, role and active flag up to date, so the policy always checks today's facts.

Notice what the solution does not do. The agent's query has no WHERE clause. The tenant check comes first, so even an admin can't reach another customer. And the agent never runs as the owner or a superuser. PGlite connects as a superuser by default, which is why the harness switches to agent with set local role. Without that line, every test would see every row.

Going further: the agent can only read here. If you let it write, add grant insert on docs to agent and a for insert policy with a WITH CHECK expression, so it can't create a document in another department. Test it the same way.

10 · Write it for IT

Now the other half of the win: the note you'd send a customer's IT team before the setup call. Imagine the customer is Acme, on Microsoft Entra ID, and the agent answers questions from Acme's documents. Write it for an IT admin, not a developer: they know SAML and SCIM better than you, but they don't know your app. It saves as you type.
