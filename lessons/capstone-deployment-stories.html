Your deployment stories

You've followed one agent from a first call at Acme to a GPU server behind Torvik's air gap. None of that is your experience yet: Acme and Torvik are made up, and the exercises ran on your laptop. This page turns what you learned into something a hiring manager can check: your own work, told as deployment stories, with every claim backed up and nothing inflated.

Now the honest part. Knowing how is not the same as having done it for a customer. The job ads in Lesson 1 ask for people who have worked with customers: it's the most common duty in FDE ads, at 55%.Bloomberry, "I analyzed 1,000 forward deployed engineer jobs". The same figure opens Lesson 2. So use the course in two ways, and keep them apart:

  • As practice you can point to. "In a practice exercise, I wrote a grader that blocks a release when one case regresses, even when the average goes up." That's true, and it's specific. Never let it sound like a deployment.
  • As a lens on your real work. You've probably done parts of these stages before without the words for them: a login you had to fit into someone else's system, an export you had to clean, a change you had to roll back. The course gives you the words and the questions. Your stories come from your own work.

2 · Rate your evidence again

In Lesson 1 you rated your proof for each stage. Do it again now, without looking, then open Lesson 1 and compare. This map saves separately, so your first one is still there.

Use the same three levels, with one stricter rule. A course exercise on its own is never strong: nobody else relied on it. It can lift a stage from thin to partial, especially if you publish your solution with notes in your own words.

strongReal work that someone relied on, which you can show or explain in detail: a repo, a write-up with numbers, a feature you can demo.
partialThe idea at small scale, or in practice: your own app, an open-source change, or a course exercise you can explain line by line.
thinNothing you could show yet.

Look at what moved. A stage that went from thin to partial through an exercise is a stage you can now talk about in detail. A stage that's strong because of real work is where your first story comes from.

3 · One story, told twice

Here is the course's own story, Torvik, told the way candidates often tell it, and then the way that holds up. (Torvik is made up, so this is only a model of the shape. Your story must be real.)

Weak

I deployed an LLM agent on-prem for a defence company, using vLLM, Kubernetes and TypeScript. I optimized the model with quantization and built a secure update system. The client was really happy with the results.

Every sentence is about you or your tools. Nothing can be checked: not "on-prem", not "optimized", not "happy". The listener learns your stack and nothing about your judgement.

Strong

Customer and pain: Torvik's field engineers waited for answers buried in sonar manuals, and nothing was allowed to leave their network.
Constraint: No internet on the inside, two 48 GB GPUs, and releases only on a disk their security officer carried across by hand.
Shipped: An import gate that refuses any release the pinned key didn't sign, and a model server sized for 16 conversations at once.
Measured: On their 12-case golden set, 11 cases passed every run, against 10 for the full-precision model, and no case got worse. The p95 time to first token was 1.35 s at the busiest hour, against a 2 s target.
Not done: I turned down the cheaper 4-bit model. It got one bolt torque wrong in one run of three.

About 125 words. The pain comes before any technology. The constraint is something the customer chose, not you. "Shipped" is two sentences. The numbers come with what they were compared against. And the last line shows judgement: you gave up something cheaper because of one case that mattered. That line usually starts the best follow-up conversation.

5 · The questions your story invites

A careful listener doesn't stop at your story. Each part of it raises a question, and each question is a stage of this course. Check that you have an answer for each one about your own project, before anyone asks.

Part of your storyThe question behind itWhere the course prepared you
Customer and painHow did you find out what hurt, and what did you leave out of scope?Lesson 2
ConstraintWho could see what, and how did you enforce it?3, 4, 11
ShippedWhat happens when the old system fails, or the agent crashes halfway through?5, 6, 7
MeasuredHow do you know it wasn't luck? What did it cost to run?8, 9
After you leftWho runs it now, and what do they do at 3 a.m.?10
Not doneWhat would you do differently with more time?All of them: the honest answer names the gap you know about

You won't have a great answer to all of them. That's normal, and saying so plainly is better than inventing one. "We never measured cost per team; next time I'd tag every model call with the team from the start" is a good answer. It shows you know what was missing.

6 · Write your story

Take the stage you rated strongest in section 2, and the real project behind it. Write it the way the strong Torvik example is written. Then write the follow-up you most hope nobody asks, and answer it. It saves in your browser as you type.

When you have one, press Copy as Markdown and keep it with your interview notes. Then do it again for the stage you rated second strongest. Two or three stories from different stages cover most of the job ads you'll read.
